Detector Characterisation

On the Thermal Behaviour of a Cooled Imaging Sensor

A worked example of report typesetting in Engraved Modernism.

Abstract

This memorandum demonstrates the system's report conventions: a thin-thick title rule, an abstract between fine rules, a single text column with marginalia, figures with small-capital references, a booktabs table and footnotes. All measurements are invented for demonstration and describe no real instrument.

1 · Introduction

Every electronic detector generates a small current in the dark. Thermal agitation lifts electrons into the conduction band at a rate that climbs steeply with temperature, and those electrons are indistinguishable from the ones produced by light. Cooling the sensor is therefore the first defence of any long exposure.1

We characterise that dependence across the operating range of a hypothetical cryostat, from −40 °C to +10 °C, and set out the consequences for exposure planning. The results appear as Fig. 1 and Table 1.

2 · Method

The sensor was held at each set-point for 30 minutes before a series of eleven dark frames, each 600 s long, was recorded. The median of the series was taken per pixel to reject cosmic-ray events, and the mean of the median frame divided by the exposure time gives the dark current.2

Below about −25 °C the dark-current shot noise in a ten-minute exposure falls beneath the 2.0 e⁻ read noise, and further cooling buys little. The cryostat's −31 °C hold point sits comfortably inside that regime.

4 · Recommendation

Operate at −30 °C or colder for exposures of ten minutes or longer. At shorter exposures the set-point may be relaxed to −20 °C, saving cryostat power with no measurable penalty.
